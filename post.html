Anatomy of an
LRT depeg.

What actually happens in the eight hours after a liquid restaking token loses its anchor — withdrawal queues, AMM curves, and the social layer.

/ BYChoi Juhwan
/ PUBLISHED2026.05.04
/ UPDATED2026.05.06
/ FIRST DRAFT14 days
/ REVIEWED BY@merklejerk

A liquid restaking token doesn't depeg the way a stablecoin depegs. The peg is to staked ETH, not to a dollar, and the mechanism is a withdrawal queue, not an over-collateralized vault. When the queue is faster than the secondary market — boring. When the secondary market is faster than the queue — interesting.

What follows is a forensic walk through one specific event, with enough numbers that you can disagree with my framing. I am going to call it the Renzo episode for brevity; the point is the mechanics, not the protocol.

§ 01The first thirty minutes

At T+0, the LRT trades at 0.9994 stETH on the deepest Curve pool. The pool has $211M of inventory split 62/38 against the underlying. Nobody panics at the four-basis-point mark. Four basis points is just bad inventory management.

At T+12, somebody — and there are exactly three wallets large enough for it to be plausibly them — moves $48M out of the pool in a single transaction. The post-trade quote is 0.9961. The pool imbalance moves to 71/29. We are now in the regime where the curve does most of the talking.

¶
// THE CORE OBSERVATION

Once inventory imbalance crosses the curve's "amp" inflection point, the marginal seller stops getting price discovery and starts getting slippage discovery. The two feel identical for the first ten minutes.

§ 02The withdrawal queue, mechanically

Most LRT issuers run a queue with a delay measured in days or weeks, not blocks. This is not a bug. The whole point of having a liquid token is so that you don't have to wait in the queue — you sell on the secondary market and let an arbitrageur eat the queue risk on your behalf.

The arbitrageur's spreadsheet is approximately:

"I can buy the LRT at 0.9961 stETH, deposit into the queue, wait N days, and redeem at 1.0000. My cost of capital is r. My probability of a worse depeg before N ends is p. If (0.0039 / N · 365) − r − p·δ > 0, I bid."

Two of those numbers are visible to everyone. Two of them aren't. The depeg widens when the unobservable ones change — usually p, occasionally r.

§ 03The social layer

Here is the part the engineers usually miss. Between T+2 and T+5, the price chart is flat-ish — and then a single Telegram message in a 14,000-person group says, "is something wrong with $TICKER?". Forty minutes later, the chart finishes drawing.

You can model this if you want. I tried. The honest answer is that the depeg has two clocks: a curve clock (deterministic, slow) and a narrative clock (binary, fast). The narrative clock dominates whenever it fires.

Things I'd want in a v2 of this writeup

  • Per-wallet attribution for the first three selling clusters.
  • A teardown of the second-largest issuer's hot path during the same window — they barely moved.
  • A real Bayesian model of how p updated based on observable on-chain signals only.
  • Something better than "the social layer dominated" as an explanation for the gap between T+5 and T+5:40.

§ 04So what?

Three takeaways, weakly held:

  1. The curve carries the first 60 bps. The narrative carries the next 200. Sizing this gap is the actual interesting research question.
  2. Withdrawal queues should be a first-class object in LRT risk dashboards. Nobody is doing this well yet. lrt-dash is my attempt.
  3. The right framing for an LRT is "stETH with credit risk", not "stETH with extra yield." If you can't price the credit, the yield is the wrong number to anchor on.

If you've worked on this and you think I have any of it wrong, email me. I read everything. I'm especially interested in talking to people who were on either side of the trade.
